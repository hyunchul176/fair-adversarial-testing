Its section "Moved from the paper" holds paragraphs that the paper gives only in summary, taken verbatim from an earlier, longer draft of the paper. ## S3.1 Baselines, reference readings and human-like drivers

Responsibility rewards are exploited through feints (Section 6.4). Re-run in B66 on all 17 SUTs, the CARS-style adversary reproduced these values exactly. Fig. 8 of the paper uses these baseline values (B23, reproduced in B66) and omits the unshielded early policy that B23 compared as its own agent. With commitment enforced at test time, without retraining (B66), the CARS-style policy no longer blamed RSS (39.2% → 0%), and, against our prediction, only 8 of its 97 collisions with the weak ladder SUTs and RSS were reference-unavoidable; its discrimination was 19.9 points (our policy stage counted the same way: 88.2; under the success contract: 87.1).  Judged by the FSM or RSS reference driver instead of C, the CARS-style adversary's collisions are 55.6% reference-unavoidable, against 51.3% under C.

Retrained with commitment, with the design fixed before the run, the CARS-style reward did not discriminate either.  From the agent's initialisation (the collision-seeking checkpoint at 0.75 M steps, then 1.5 M steps) it collided with the weakest SUT in 89.8% of cases and with longitudinal RSS in 75.3%, but C avoided only 1.1% and 0% of these attacks, so P1 was 1.1 points [0.0, 3.2], below the threshold of 50 fixed before the run (CB1); from scratch (2.25 M steps) P1 was 0.5 [0.0, 1.6] (CB1b). C could not avoid 99.5% and 99.8% of its collisions with the weak ladder SUTs and RSS, and the policy also collided with the competent SUTs in about half of the cases, always reference-unavoidable. Behind our shield it caused no reference-unavoidable collision but found fair failures of the weakest SUT in only 18.8% and 9.7% of the cases (success contract: 14.5% and 7.5%). Both trainings reached a contact rate of 0.79–0.94 within the first 0.25 M steps and stayed there. Our policy, rerun in the same evaluation, reproduced the stored policy stage action for action (3,162 of 3,162 episodes).

Feasibility references blame competent SUTs (Section 6.4), and shielding each policy with its own reference does not change this (B66, BL3 supported). The zero-reaction shield changed 755 actions in 312 of 3,162 episodes and the omniscient shield none, and discrimination stayed at 1.6 points, with 98.8–98.9% of the collisions with weak SUTs and RSS reference-unavoidable. An ideal driver avoids nearly every cut-in, so a shield built on it filters almost nothing.

A second implementation of the regulatory models (Section 6.3) changes the verdicts mainly where the reference's reaction timing matters (Table S5). Counting only failures that \(C_\mathrm{CARS}\) also avoids lowers the detection of the weak ladder SUTs from 74.3 to 66.3% (development) and from 59.2 to 50.1% (MiTra), and that of longitudinal RSS from 12.2 to 5.8% (MiTra): about half of MiTra's RSS failures depend on when the reference starts reacting. \(\mathrm{FSM}_\mathrm{CARS}\) avoids 23–40% of the collisions that C cannot avoid, so for these collisions a test judged by that FSM blames the SUT more often than one judged by C.

The population of human-like drivers (Section 6.3) consists of 100 attentive drivers. Each has C's structure with its own parameters, sampled from published distributions (perception threshold 0.375–1.095 m, reaction median 0.75 s, braking 7.0 ± 1.0 m/s², emergency TTC 1.5–3 s; braking only). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) between the reported failures and the collisions that C cannot avoid is 0.987 on the development cases and 0.977 [0.968, 0.985] on MiTra. For the earlier version, the median avoidance of its reported failures, the median avoidance of the collisions that C cannot avoid and the AUC between the two on the development cases were 91%, 0% and 0.989. Among the baseline collisions that C cannot avoid, those of the CARS-style adversary are avoided by a median 69% of the drivers on the development cases and 76% on MiTra, and those of the Dynasto-style adversary by 21% on MiTra. Drivers who perceive the cut-in earlier than C often avoid them, so for these baselines the reference-unavoidable verdict is conservative. The collision-seeking adversary's collisions remain unavoidable (median 0%). A less attentive population (reaction median 1.25 s, braking 5.5 m/s²) avoids few of the agent's failures (median 6% and 14%).

## S3.2 MiTra and TGSIM

MiTra (172 cut-ins from nine flights) evaluated the earlier version v2j, whose policy stage is the final agent's, under a frozen protocol without deviations.  Discrimination P1 (following H0 − longitudinal RSS, 37 queries) was 62.0 percentage points [54.7, 69.2], which meets the criterion of at least 50 on the point estimate. The three seeds gave 62.2, 61.6 and 62.2, and the flight-cluster interval [46.5, 75.4] includes the threshold. For P2 (longitudinal and lateral RSS and the delayed learned SUTs), longitudinal RSS failed in 2.9% of the cases [0.6, 5.8] and the others in 0%, which meets the criterion of at most 5% each on the point estimates, although the upper bound for RSS exceeds 5%. P3 is a property of the baseline, not of the agent: 99.8% of the 950 collisions of the collision-seeking agent with competent SUTs were reference-unavoidable, which meets the criterion of at least 80%.

Detection within 37 queries was 64.9% for following H0, 64.5% for IDM, 51.4% for H0.5, 23.3% for H1, 51.9% for BC-highD and 40–50% for the learned lane-assignment SUTs, and Kendall's τ with capability on the training-panel ladder was −0.976. AEB failed in 11.6–17.4% of the cases, whereas its rate was 0% on the development cases and at most 2% on the reserved highD recordings. A post-hoc interpretation, which we have not tested, is that MiTra cut-ins are denser and faster and that a TTC-triggered AEB responds late to short-gap cut-ins (Section S5). Post hoc, without the failures closing below 3 m/s, P1 is 45.9 [39.0, 52.7], below the threshold.

The final agent was evaluated on MiTra post hoc, with the reuse labelled.  Its screening removed every inadmissible execution, whereas the unscreened refinement had spent 45% of its SUT queries on them, and C could not avoid 4,607 of the SUT collisions during that search. Detection within 37 queries rose by 12.2 points on average over the nine detectable SUTs, in the same direction for all nine (following H0 68.0%, IDM 66.3%, H0.5 58.3%, H1 44.2%, BC-highD 54.7%, ADS 2.0 m 54.1%, learned lane-assignment 61.0–66.7%), and P1 would be 55.8 percentage points [48.3, 63.4]. As for severity, 39.7% of its failures on the weak ladder SUTs close below 3 m/s. Without them P1 is 43.4 [36.4, 50.4], and with the 3 m/s floor in the search (v2s-S) it is 53.1 [45.4, 60.7]. The agent also found more failures in competent SUTs, AEB in 23.8–30.2% of the cases and longitudinal RSS in 12.2%, where the earlier version had found 11.6–17.4% and 2.9%. Of the 119 case–SUT pairs with a failure on a competent SUT, 87 (73%) were consensus-fair, and about half of the RSS failures depend on when the reference starts reacting (Table S5). Counting only reported failures that are not consensus-fair, the five SUTs of the specificity criterion are at 2.3% (longitudinal RSS), 0.6% (lateral RSS) and 0% (delayed learned SUTs).

TGSIM I-294 (80 cases) was also evaluated post hoc, with the reuse labelled.  The final agent found failures in 81.3% of the cases for the weakest SUT and 1.3% for longitudinal RSS (earlier version: 77.9% and 0%), so P1 would be 80.0 percentage points. Screening removed every inadmissible execution, whereas the unscreened refinement spent 54% of its SUT queries on them, and detection rose by 15.3 points on average over the nine detectable SUTs, in the same direction for all nine. Of the 25 competent-SUT failures, 21 (84%) were consensus-fair, and all were graded avoidable.

## S3.6 Attack authority

The detection of the weakest SUT (following H0) within 37 SUT queries depends on the adversary's speed headroom, the SUT's initial speed minus the adversary's 15 m/s speed floor, which bounds how far the adversary can slow down in front of the SUT (Section 6.3). With less than 2 m/s of headroom, the earlier version, with the unscreened refinement, found failures of the weakest SUT in only 4.0% of the MiTra and 5.7% of the NGSIM cases (post hoc; TGSIM below 2 m/s has two cases).

## S4.1 Autoware Universe

On Autoware Universe (Section 6.5), the policy stage ran first, and the screened refinement then ran on the cases in which the policy missed for at least one seed; the designs were fixed before each run. The policy was frozen before the first Autoware run, whereas the screening was designed after it, on these cases.

The earlier version, with the unscreened refinement, confirmed 90.9% and 66.7% in its first run, a difference of 24.2 points [15.6, 33.3]. Its search exposed Autoware to 128 reference-unavoidable collisions, none of them reported. It was rerun on the same instances, cases and random streams on the day of the screened refinement, and it then confirmed 88.7% and 66.1% and executed 56% of its 1,358 queries on inadmissible plans; C could not avoid 152 of the Autoware collisions among them. Against this same-day rerun, screening raised confirmed detection by 2.2 points [0.0, 4.8] and 11.3 points [5.4, 17.7]. Each refinement success cost 25.6 and 34.7 Autoware queries instead of 64.5 and 243, but more instance time (Section S1.4), and the screened refinement's own failures reproduced in 90% and 78% of their reruns.  The unscreened refinement varied by 0.5–2.2 points between its two runs.

The cause test used the earlier version with the unscreened refinement, and its design was fixed before the run.  In the earlier version's first run, Autoware's braking jerk in AW-r157 was a median −1.45 m/s³. Lifting only the planner's comfort limits (−1.0 m/s², −1.0 m/s³, raised to C's values) reduced confirmed failures from 66.7% to 58.1% [47.3, 68.3], short of the halving fixed before the run. These failures braked later but harder, and stopped at the controller's shipped limit. Lifting the controller limits as well left 2.2% [0, 5.9], and 2.4% on the two seeds unaffected by a start-up fault of one instance. No collision was reference-unavoidable in either setting. Braking limits at two layers therefore account for nearly all confirmed failures in these cut-ins. Braking onset also shifted when the limits were lifted, so the limits and the planner's timing interact, and this causal reading rests on our parameter intervention, not on the agent. The written interpretation deviates from the rule fixed before the run for this outcome ("the decision logic also contributes"). That rule did not use the descriptive controller-limit comparison, and the deviation is disclosed in the result document.

The perception-latency test had its design fixed before the run and amended before the run, as described below.  The braking-competent configuration (both limits lifted) was given a latency-compensated perception delay, in which each delayed measurement of the adversary is extrapolated to the present at its own velocity. A 0.6 s delay produced confirmed fair failures in 3.8% [1.1, 7.0] of the cases at the policy stage, whereas a 0.3 s delay (0.5%) was indistinguishable from none (0%). Reported failures were 9.1, 3.8 and 4.3%, of which 34% or fewer reproduced, and no collision was reference-unavoidable. The amendment replaced an uncompensated delay, which placed the adversary about 15 m behind its true position and suppressed braking altogether in a smoke test, as a straw man.

Perception noise and missed detections were tested on the braking-competent configuration, again with the design fixed before the run.  Gaussian noise on the adversary object (0.5 m along the lane, 0.3 m across, 0.5 m/s) raised confirmed fair failures from 0% to 1.1% [0.0, 2.7], which we read as no clear effect. Missed-detection bursts (0.5 s, 20% of ticks, the object coasting at constant velocity) gave 0.5% [0.0, 1.6]. The hypothesis, fixed before the run, that they raise detection was rejected, because a tracker that coasts through misses avoids nearly all of these cut-ins; braking onset did not move (median 0.9 s in both). Reported failures (7.5–12.9%) reproduced in only 2.4–8.3% of their reruns, and no collision was reference-unavoidable under any perception fault.

The minimal-risk manoeuvre engaged in 4.1% of the AW-r157 failures and 14.4% of the AW-default failures. The closed-loop shield occasionally took longer than one 0.1 s tick (maximum 0.64 s). Confirmation by open-loop reruns controls for this, and excluding all affected failures leaves 85.5% and 59.7% in the first run.

Post hoc, the logged states give the closing speed at contact, Autoware's speed minus the adversary's longitudinal speed at the first tick without clearance, as for the highway-env SUTs.  The agent's confirmed failures closed at a median 5.13 m/s (10th–90th percentile 3.68–7.42, at most 12.4) with as-shipped braking, 2.4% of the 165 below 3 m/s and 55% at 5 m/s or more, and at 3.56 m/s (1.97–5.35, at most 6.9) for AW-r157, 34.1% of the 132 below 3 m/s and 17% at 5 m/s or more. All were rear-end contacts in the SUT's lane. Over the 113 policy-stage failures confirmed under both variants for the same case and seed (47 cases), as-shipped braking closed a median 1.51 m/s faster [1.29, 1.64] (case bootstrap), faster in 95% of the pairs. The closed-loop policy need not produce the same trajectory under both settings and the pairs condition on two failures, so this is an association consistent with the braking-limit diagnosis, not the effect of the limits on a fixed attack. The refinement's failures were lighter (AW-r157: 12 of its 16 below 3 m/s).

Post hoc on the 172 MiTra cut-ins, the earlier version confirmed failures in 58.5% [51.2, 65.7] of the cases with as-shipped braking and 36.2% [29.7, 43.2] for AW-r157, a difference of 22.3 percentage points [16.9, 27.9]. The failure mode was the same, and the highway-env ladder's rates are also lower on MiTra. None of the 403 policy-stage collisions was reference-unavoidable (collision-seeking: 649 of 653).

## S4.2 Third-party SUTs

The four third-party SUTs (Section 6.5) passed the implementation checks. None of them collided in the 62 attack-free cut-ins, no policy-stage collision was reference-unavoidable, and the refinement executed no inadmissible plan (0 of 9,821 queries). The collision-seeking baseline collided, with one query (seed means), in 59.7% of the cases with the CommonRoad planner and in 85.5% with each SUMO model, and all 588 of its collisions (three seeds, four SUTs) were reference-unavoidable (B69-T2). The attribution problem is therefore not an artefact of our SUTs.

For the SUMO models, the final agent found fair failures in 3.2%, 1.6% and 3.2% of the cases (ACC, Krauss, IDM), all through the refinement, and its policy collided in none of 558 episodes. This is the level of our competent SUTs (2.2%). The case found for all three closed at 4.9–8.6 m/s, while C avoided it by 6.8–7.9 m. The models braked harder than 1 m/s² only 3.5–3.7 s after the cut-in became visible, because SUMO assigns a leader only once the cut-in vehicle's centre crosses the lane line.

For the CommonRoad planner, detection was 33.3% [25.3, 41.4] at one query and 73.1% [61.8, 83.9] at 37 queries. All 136 reported failures closed below 3 m/s (median 0.22 m/s). We read this as the planner's default design: with no safety margin and a constant-velocity prediction, it brakes just enough to clear the overlap it predicts, and so touches a vehicle that keeps braking. With the severity floor (v2s-S), the search found severe failures in 6.5% [1.6, 12.9] of the cases.

Reference agreement differed between the two kinds of SUT. For the SUMO models, 0–1.6% of the cases had a reported failure that was not consensus-fair, but for the planner it was 9.7% [4.8, 15.1], above the 5% criterion (B69-T3 rejected for this SUT). ## S5. Ablations, controls, cost and extended discussion

The guards were ablated on the earlier unshielded policy (seed 1). Without G1, the RSS SUT is blamed in 74–76% of the cases, entirely through feints, and without G2 only 28% of the fair failures are realistic cut-ins. Only with both does the agent achieve discrimination, specificity and realism together.

The advantage of learning comes from traffic with attack authority; in low-authority NGSIM traffic it vanishes (6.4% versus 6.4%).

The controls were run on the development cases with the design fixed before the run.  SUTs built from C with one factor changed give detection in the known order of capability. C itself and the two variants stronger than C in one factor (reaction 0.5 s, braking 8.5 m/s²) are never detected, at any budget. Along each degraded factor, the agent's detection within 37 queries rises monotonically: a reaction delay of +0.25, +0.5 and +1.0 s gives 86.0, 91.9 and 93.5%, braking limits of 6.5 and 5.5 m/s² give 41.4 and 83.3%, and perception at 1.5 and 2.0 m gives 90.3 and 91.9%. The weakest level's lower bound exceeds 74% in all three factors, and the policy stage alone follows the same order. Reaction and perception saturate at their first step (86.0 and 90.3%), so only braking shows a graded response. On the 120 exiD cut-ins the same controls keep this order (Section S13.7).

The shield alone does not make a fair adversary. The collision-seeking policy, run through the same shield, has no reference-unavoidable collision (0 of 3,162), but it finds fair failures with one query in only 5.4% of the cases for the weakest SUTs (the agent's policy: 87.1%), and its discrimination between the weakest SUT and longitudinal RSS is 5.4 points (the agent's policy: 87.1). The shield blocks most of its attacks on the weak followers (13 collisions in 186 episodes). Against BC-highD and the learned SUTs it collides often, but mostly with the adversary less than 3.5 m ahead, so those collisions are not robust failures. The fairness property comes from the shield, whereas one-query detection and discrimination come from the fairness reward.

The model in the search matters as well. Screened random search under the same admission rule finds 48.4% within 36 queries for the weakest SUT (screened BO: 87.1%) and 3.2% for ADS 2.0 m (69.4%). Attack-free cut-ins produce no failure: continuing each natural cut-in at constant speed collided with none of the 17 SUTs in any of the 62 cases, so the failures that the agent reports are created by the attack.

**Where learning helps.** The learned policy acts as an amortised search. Where the traffic leaves attack authority (highD), one policy query replaces about 20 screened-search queries for SUTs like its training panel, and on unseen naturalistic data the factor falls to 12 (TGSIM) and 2 (MiTra). On the unseen Autoware stack the policy produced most reported failures with one query, while each further search success cost 26–35 Autoware queries. Its one-query skill did not extend to SUTs of other designs, even after retraining against a population of drivers of C's family, and for those SUTs the screened search is the efficient tool. Both stages are fair at execution time. The shield alone does not provide detection: a collision-seeking policy behind the same shield stays fair but finds one-query failures in only 5.4% of the cases for the weakest SUTs. The fairness reward is what teaches the policy to produce front-contact failures that C avoids.

**Why a human-like reference, and which reading.** Feasibility references accept attacks that a human could not survive, and rule-based validity accepts collisions that a careful driver could not avoid; shielding a feasibility-referenced adversary with its own reference does not repair this. The verdicts hold under held-out references and an independent implementation of the regulatory models, with one reading-dependent part. When the reference starts its reaction only once the TTC is below 2 s, 18% of the reported failures become unavoidable, half of MiTra's longitudinal RSS failures among them. Our reading follows the regulation's description of perception and emergency braking (Section 3.2), and we report both. A sampled population of attentive human-like drivers gives the same verdicts for the final agent (its failures avoided by a median 91–96% of the drivers; AUC 0.977–0.987), except that these drivers often avoid collisions of the CARS- and Dynasto-style baselines that C cannot avoid. Drivers who notice the cut-in later than C avoid fewer than half of the reported failures, so the fairness claim holds for drivers at least as attentive as C. The reference itself must be audited: the literal time-based R157 text brakes for natural wandering, and both regulatory models contain gaps at the boundaries of their perception and risk conditions.

**Fairness depends on the scenario.** Plans that make C itself collide were found in 71–82% of the cut-in cases, 22% of the cut-out cases and none of the deceleration cases. The cut-in figure is a grid lower bound for a reaction variant of C and is not strictly comparable. The more the attacker controls when and where a collision becomes visible, the larger the fairness problem, and a collision-seeking adversary's low reference-unavoidable share (3% for cut-outs) is no guarantee.

**Deceleration and cut-out scenarios.** These scenarios were generated in a one-dimensional longitudinal simulation and only re-executed in CARLA vehicle physics, where most verdicts held. In the deceleration scenarios the CARLA SUT collides in 93% of the agent's robust failures, and C, with its regulatory AEB layer, avoids all of them; in the cut-out scenarios 96.5% of C's avoidances hold.  Rerun with the corrected CARLA harness (Section S4.3), the SUT collided in 91.5% of the deceleration failures, and C avoided all of them even without its AEB layer. In the cut-out scenarios C avoided all 400 and the SUT collided in 90.0% (Section S13.5).

**S6.6 Contact geometry.** We replayed the final agent's reported failures for the weakest SUT and the eight competent SUTs on the development, MiTra, HIGH-SIM and exiD cases and for the CommonRoad planner, with the design fixed before the run.  Each contact was classified from the overlap of the two body rectangles in the pre-impact states of the first contact tick, and each failure was replayed with both bodies shrunk by 5 and 10 cm per side. All 1,096 replays reproduced the contact, and the replays without shrinking reproduced the stored runs (1,080 of 1,080) with identical trajectories before the contact. Of the competent SUTs' 564 seed-level reported failures, 86.2% still collide when both bodies shrink by 5 cm (75.5% at 10 cm), which supports the hypothesis fixed before the run (GA1, at least 80%) that most pooled contacts survive this tolerance; it does not show that every contact is physically exact. They persist at 5 cm in 90.9% of the development, 89.9% of the MiTra, 87.5% of the HIGH-SIM and 73.8% of the exiD failures. Each contact is typed by the body region nearest to the overlap. At the contact tick, 423 of the 552 classified seed-level contacts are rear-end (the SUT's front against the adversary's rear) and 129 (23.4%) are side to side, the adversary's side touching the side of the SUT's front section; none touches the adversary's side with the SUT's front. The 3.5 m criterion of the robust verdict is therefore a longitudinal front-position criterion, not a guarantee of a frontal contact. The contact tick is the first 10 Hz state with overlap, so the bodies have already interpenetrated; at the first touch, interpolated linearly in position, heading and speed between the last free tick and the contact tick (an estimate, not a re-integration), 66 of the 552 (12.0%) are side contacts and the median closing speed along the contact normal is 2.51 m/s instead of 1.54 m/s. **S6.7 Cut-in style.** The agent's attacks have one style, which the figures and videos show and which the realism contract allows but natural traffic rarely produces (post hoc, stored rows; B78 pre-run analysis). The features are measured on C's replay, as for the realism contract.  At the 50% lateral crossing, 57.5% of the 463 natural highD cut-ins of the realism reference are faster than the follower, that is, they overtake it, and the cut-in vehicles hardly change speed (median mean acceleration +0.11 m/s² in the 3 s before the crossing and +0.10 m/s² in the 2 s after it; 4.8% brake harder than −0.5 m/s² after it). Only 0.65% are slower than the follower and brake after the crossing. In the 62 development cases, 29% of the recorded cut-in vehicles were slower than the follower at the start of the lane change. The policy stage's reported failures on the weak ladder (463 seed-level failures) all enter slower than the follower (median 6.3 m/s), with a median mean acceleration of −1.18 m/s² before the crossing, and 78% brake harder than −0.5 m/s² after it; 95% of the refinement's 395 first successes are slower cut-ins as well. Of the policy-stage failures, 54% brake harder before the crossing than the natural 1st percentile (−1.12 m/s²) and 20% harder after it than its 1st percentile (−1.26 m/s²), which the G2 tolerance of one IQR beyond those percentiles allows (before the crossing down to −1.58 m/s²). The style follows from the fairness condition: a cut-in vehicle that overtakes the follower opens the gap, so it can provoke a collision only by braking later. Keeping only the stored failures whose two accelerations stay at or above the natural 1st percentile leaves P1 at 58.1 points [47.3, 68.8] (weak ladder 39.7%), and at or above the 5th percentile 5.4 [0.5, 11.3] (3.5%). These filter the stored searches; searched again with the bounds in the screening, P1 is 88.7 and 74.2 points, and starting behind the follower again produces slower cut-ins (Sections S13.3 and S13.4).

## S13. Follow-up experiments

The experiments of this section were run after the review of the earlier drafts, each with its design and analysis fixed before its run (Table S11, rows 35 and 36); none changes the agent or an earlier verdict. The development cases are in-sample, and the runs on exiD and the other datasets reuse their data post hoc. Sections 6.2, 6.3 and 6.5 of the paper summarise them.

### S13.1 Stricter realism, searched again (B75)

The refinement was rerun on the 62 development cases × 17 SUTs with stricter realism in its screening and success contract: the joint region at its 95th percentile (τ = 1.643, condition q95) or half the G2 tolerance (0.5 IQR, condition g05); 1,054 searches each without errors, after a pre-check that reproduced the stored values at the contract. The agent's P1 was 82.8 points [73.1, 91.4] under q95 and 88.2 [80.1, 95.2] under g05, against 11.3 and 74.2 from filtering the stored searches (RS1 and RS2, threshold 50, both supported; Table S8). Under q95 the policy's single query rarely qualified (weak ladder 0.8%), so the refinement found almost all failures, at 459 C replays per SUT query instead of 142. The learned lane-assignment SUTs lost most of their failures (37 queries: 14.5 and 7.0% against 62.5% at the contract). The competent SUTs failed in 1.4 and 3.0% of the cases on average; the exception, AEB H0 under g05 (11.3%, 7 cases), was consensus-fair in every case and graded avoidable, and six of the seven failures closed at 1 m/s or less (post-hoc re-reading of all 22 competent-SUT failures with the held-out references). The ladder kept its order in both conditions.

### S13.2 Margin and severity frontier (B76)

Four refinement-only conditions on the development cases × 9 SUTs, each run after a pre-check that reproduced the stored searches (27 of 27 pairs), varied C's margin (0.25 and 1.0 m against the contract's 0.5 m) and the severity floor (5 and 7 m/s against 3 m/s). The hypothesis that a larger margin lowers the closing speed of the reported failures of following H0.5 and H1, BC-highD and ADS 2.0 m was rejected (FS1): their median closing speed was 3.23 m/s at 1.0 m against 2.46 m/s at 0.25 m (2.50 at 0.5 m). Severe-only detection of these SUTs was lower at 1.0 m, as predicted (FS2), but by 3.2 points (28.2 versus 31.5%); the larger margin mainly reduced how many failures were found (refinement successes 292 → 246 pairs; ADS 2.0 m 80.6 → 33.9%). The severity floor trades detection for severity: the agent's P1 was 88.7 [80.6, 95.7] at 3 m/s, 63.4 [52.2, 74.2] at 5 m/s and 32.3 [21.0, 43.5] at 7 m/s (median closing speed 4.8, 6.5 and 8.0 m/s), while BC-highD kept 71% at 7 m/s. The AEB variants failed in 1.6–6.5% of the cases at every margin.

### S13.3 Naturalistic braking bounds, searched again (B78)

The refinement was rerun on the development cases against the four weak ladder SUTs and longitudinal RSS with the cut-in vehicle's mean acceleration in the 3 s before and the 2 s after the crossing bounded below by the natural highD 1st percentile (−1.12 and −1.26 m/s², a1) or 5th percentile (−0.47 and −0.49 m/s², a5), in the screening and the success contract; pre-checks reproduced the stored searches (15 of 15 pairs), with 310 searches per condition and no errors. P1 was 88.7 points [80.7, 95.7] under a1 and 74.2 [62.9, 85.5] under a5, against 58.1 and 5.4 from filtering the stored failures (NB1 and NB2, threshold 50, both supported). Within 37 queries, a1 gave 88.7, 85.5, 67.7, 24.2 and 1.6% for following H0, IDM, following H0.5, following H1 and longitudinal RSS, and a5 74.2, 75.8, 46.8, 11.3 and 0%. Under a5 the policy's single query almost never qualified (weak ladder 0.3%), C was replayed 290 times per SUT query, and the refinement's successes were slower cut-ins at nearly constant speed: 99.2% crossed slower than the follower (median 5.6 m/s), with a median acceleration of −0.39 m/s² before and +0.33 m/s² after the crossing, and none braked harder than 0.5 m/s² after it.

### S13.4 Cut-ins started behind the follower (B79)

To test whether the attacks must be slower cut-ins, the screened search was started from 69 highD cases in which the cut-in vehicle begins 6 m or more behind the follower and a median 7 m/s faster (18 development and 51 calibration recordings' cases), with the adversary's acceleration bound raised to +2 m/s², against six SUTs; the design and four predictions were fixed before the run, and every job passed the replay gate and a pre-check against the stored searches. Table S15 gives the fair failures within 36 queries. The screened GP-BO found fair failures of following H0 and IDM in 46.4% of the cases, against 8.7% for screened random search (P4 supported), and of BC-highD in 81.2% (P2 supported), while longitudinal RSS stayed at 0–1.6% in every condition (P3 supported). The rule-based followers' failures were again slower cut-ins: 84 of the 88 of both searches (95.5%) crossed slower than the follower, after passing it and braking while changing lanes (median acceleration before the crossing −1.34 m/s² for following H0 in the screened GP-BO search), so the prediction that most of them brake after the crossing was rejected (69.3%, P1). Requiring the cut-in vehicle to be at least as fast as the follower at the crossing removed the following controllers' fair failures (0% from behind, 1.6% from the development starts) but left IDM (14.5%) and BC-highD (84.1%). Bounding the braking by the natural 1st or 5th percentile left 33.3 or 17.4% for following H0 and 31.9 or 26.1% for IDM, with longitudinal RSS at 0. By the interpretation rule fixed before the run, the outcome is intermediate: overtaking cut-ins are not the main mode for any rule-based SUT (at most 4.3% of the cases under the plain search), but they exist for IDM and dominate for BC-highD, which brakes like the highD drivers it imitates and has no emergency layer. Faster cut-ins open the gap, so within what C avoids they leave a follower that reacts after the cut-in little room to fail.

### S13.5 The agent in CARLA

These experiments replace the CARLA evidence of an earlier prototype with runs of the agent itself, without retraining, in the set-up of Section S4.3. Each had its design and decision rules fixed before its run (Table S11, row 36), and all results below were obtained with the corrected harness; Table S12 lists the judged criteria. Closed-loop runs and searches use the 62 development cases (in-sample). Replays of stored attacks also use TGSIM, MiTra, HIGH-SIM and exiD, a post-hoc reuse of these data.

**Closed loop.** Each of the three policy seeds made one shielded closed-loop query per case against eight CARLA SUTs: following H0, AEB H0, longitudinal and lateral RSS, BehaviorAgent with its cautious, normal and aggressive profiles, and the Traffic Manager. This gives 1,488 runs per condition. With the shield replaying C in the matched twin, no run ended in a reference-unavoidable collision. Following H0 had robust fair failures in 86.6% [78.5, 93.5] of the cases (seeds 82.3, 90.3 and 87.1%) and BehaviorAgent in 3.8, 5.9 and 7.0%, while AEB H0, the two RSS SUTs and the Traffic Manager had none. Adding G2 and joint realism, computed on the reference replay in the default twin, kept 84.9% for following H0 and 3.2, 5.4 and 6.5% for BehaviorAgent. G2 removed no attack, and joint realism removed three of following H0 and three of BehaviorAgent. These two conditions need the adversary's actions, which we reconstructed from its logged trajectory. Twenty-six runs reconstructed with errors of 0.01–0.02 m, because the matched twin's body length shifts highway-env's kinematics by 1–2 cm, and no verdict changed. A repeated run of seed 1 reproduced all 496 verdicts. Without the shield, 99 runs were reference-unavoidable, 96 of them with BehaviorAgent, and following H0 failed in 79.6%. With the shield replaying C in the default twin, 4 runs were reference-unavoidable and following H0 failed in 80.1%. The learned baselines of Section 6.4, run without a shield, collided in 85.0% (collision seeking with commitment), 81.3% (CARS-style) and 85.4% (Dynasto-style) of the runs. Of these collisions 99.5, 98.2 and 84.0% were reference-unavoidable, and the baselines had robust fair failures of following H0 in 0, 2.2 and 0% of the cases.

**Searches inside CARLA.** The screened refinement was run from the boundary plan with every SUT query in CARLA, 36 queries per case and SUT. The screening replayed C either in the matched twin or in the default twin, and both arms and their repeat ran on the same machine. With matched-twin screening, it found failures of following H0 in 87.1% [79.0, 95.2] of the cases and of BehaviorAgent in 50.0, 51.6 and 62.9%. AEB H0 and longitudinal RSS failed in one case each (1.6%), and lateral RSS and the Traffic Manager in none. Replayed with CARLA's C, all 158 successes were avoided, 155 of them with C's 0.5 m margin. With default-twin screening, following H0 failed in 83.9% and CARLA's C collided in 5 of the 156 successes (3.2%). We repeated the matched-twin searches of following H0 and of BehaviorAgent with its normal profile, 124 searches with the same random streams. They gave the same success or failure in 95.2% of them, and both success rates were 1.6 points lower. A search for SUT collisions without regard to fairness ran GP-BO from the centre of the plan box with the same budget and stopped at the first collision. It found collisions in 82.3–87.1% of the cases for every SUT. Of the 209 collisions with AEB H0, the two RSS SUTs and the Traffic Manager, 93.3% were reference-unavoidable, and none met the success contract. For following H0, 70.4% of its collisions were reference-unavoidable and one case met the contract; for BehaviorAgent, 68.5–69.8% were reference-unavoidable and one or two cases met it. The agent's search found 52 and 31–38 such cases that CARLA's C confirmed with its margin.

**Replays of stored attacks.** Every failure that the agent reported within 37 queries on a dataset was replayed once in CARLA, unchanged, against the same SUT and against C. A stored attack transfers if CARLA's SUT collides and CARLA's C avoids it. For following H0 the transfer was 90.6% on the development cases (seeds 91.2, 94.7 and 86.0%), meeting the criterion of at least 90% fixed before the run. On TGSIM, MiTra, HIGH-SIM and exiD it was 85.1, 72.4, 80.6 and 83.5%, below the same criterion for each dataset. CARLA's C collided in 0, 2.1, 1.1, 4.3 and 0.3% of the attacks on the five datasets, and CARLA's SUT avoided 9.4, 12.8, 26.5, 17.2 and 16.2%. The IDM follower transferred in 90.1, 81.8, 71.3, 83.9 and 83.2%, following H0.5 in 31.6–40.6% and following H1 in 9.3–25.0%. The refinement's attacks, which lie near C's avoidance boundary by design, transferred less often than the policy's (development cases, all SUTs pooled: 19.6 against 77.5%). A second replay of the 514 development trajectories agreed in 99.0% of the transfer verdicts. Keeping the lateral distance to the SUT's lane centre in metres instead of scaling it lowered the transfer for following H0 to 53.3–66.7% on the five datasets (criterion: at least 90%). C then perceives the cut-in only after 0.5 m more lateral motion. With a Tesla Model 3 for all three vehicles, the transfer on the development cases was 59.6% (criterion: within 5 points of the Mustang). CARLA's C avoided every attack, and the wider SUT avoided 40.4% of them. With the earlier harness these transfers had been higher, 94.2% for following H0 on the development cases and 81.1–92.4% on the other datasets, partly because CARLA's SUTs had braked less than requested.

**Geometry and physics.** To separate geometry from physics, the lost attacks were replayed again with C and the SUT perceiving the scene in highway-env's geometry while their vehicles kept CARLA's physics. All 265 attacks in which CARLA's C had collided were then avoided, so geometry explains every loss on C's side. Of the 1,101 attacks that CARLA's SUT had avoided, geometry explained 60% for the weak SUTs and 47% for the AEB and RSS SUTs. The remainder includes the shorter CARLA bodies, which this check does not change. In a control sample of 200 transferred attacks, the same replay changed 2 SUT verdicts and no C verdict.

**Matched twin and re-check.** Replaying the 3,207 stored attacks of the five datasets in the matched twin predicted CARLA's C collision verdict in 96.0% of them, against 91.7% for the default twin. The criterion was at least 95% and above the default twin. The matched twin passed 75.3% of the attacks, that is, C avoided them there by at least 0.5 m, and none of these made CARLA's C collide (criterion: at most 3%). Under the default twin, which passes every stored attack, 8.3% did. The matched twin also agreed with CARLA on whether C kept its 0.5 m margin in 95.6% of the attacks (default twin 79.6%). Only one attack it passed fell short of the margin in CARLA, against 653 under the default twin. The twin matched to the distance-preserving mapping agreed in 96.1% (default twin 42.9%), passed 26.9% and let through none that CARLA's C could not avoid; 94.7% of the attacks it passed transferred. The twin matched to the Tesla agreed in 95.5%, passed 78.8% and let through none. It did not, however, exceed the default twin's 96.9%, because CARLA's C rarely collided with the Tesla (3.1%), so that part of the criterion was not met.

The SUT's side was examined after the first replays, with criteria fixed before the computation. Replaying the SUT models in the matched twin predicted CARLA's SUT collision verdict in 94.8, 84.2, 82.0 and 93.9% of the attacks for following H0, H0.5 and H1 and the IDM follower. Pooled over these four SUTs it agreed in 90.6%, against 68.5% for the default twin (criterion: at least 90% and above the default twin). Keeping only the attacks of following H0 that remained fair failures in the matched twin, with C's 0.5 m margin, left 782 of 980 trajectories, of which 93.7% transferred (criterion: at least 90%), against 84.1% of all 980. Counted by seed, case and SUT, the re-checked transfer was 91.4, 97.8, 92.8, 95.8 and 92.6% on the development cases, TGSIM, MiTra, HIGH-SIM and exiD, keeping 89, 71, 67, 76 and 79% of the attacks. For the IDM follower it was 92.7, 96.4, 89.8, 95.8 and 91.3%. Following H0.5 kept only 22–30% of its attacks and 64–80% of these transferred. On the Tesla, with the twin matched to it, 62% were kept and 80.2% of these transferred. The twin matched to the Tesla predicted the Tesla SUT's verdict for following H0 in 86.8% of the attacks, against 61.7% for the twin matched to the Mustang, which locates the Tesla's loss in its wider body. Re-checking only C's side, as a black-box SUT would require, removed every attack that CARLA's C could not avoid but left a transfer of 80.1% for following H0.

**A wider vehicle.** With the Tesla for all three vehicles and the shield replaying C in the twin matched to it, the closed-loop policy had no reference-unavoidable collision. It found robust fair failures of following H0 in 67.2% [57.5, 75.3] of the cases, below the 75% fixed before the run; BehaviorAgent failed in 2.2–2.7%, and the other four SUTs had no robust fair failure. The twin matched to the Mustang, applied to the Tesla, also gave no reference-unavoidable collision and 66.1% for following H0, while BehaviorAgent's nine fair failures per profile all fell short of C's margin. Because both twins place C's perception point identically, the criterion that matching the twin to the Tesla reduces reference-unavoidable collisions could not be met. With the earlier harness this comparison had given 0 against 10 such collisions, all due to the Tesla C's actuation.

To see whether training closes this gap, we trained a policy with the agent's recipe and starting checkpoints but with the vehicle width drawn uniformly from 1.9 to 2.7 m in every episode. Development had set this variant aside for its small effect (Table S11, row 5). After the Tesla results it was first probed with one seed; its seeds 2 and 3 were then trained, and all three were run with criteria fixed before the runs. On the Tesla it found robust fair failures of following H0 in 73.1% [64.5, 80.6] of the cases, 5.9 points above the agent's policy (per-seed differences of +9, −1 and +3 cases), short of the 8 points required. On the Mustang it found 84.9% [76.9, 91.9], 1.7 points below the agent's policy, within the 5 points allowed. Neither vehicle had a reference-unavoidable collision or a robust fair failure of AEB H0, the RSS SUTs or the Traffic Manager. In highway-env, with 2.66 m wide vehicles and the shield, both policies fell to about 64% for following H0 (64.5 and 62.9%, against 88.2 and 87.6% at 2.0 m). The variant found more failures of BC-highD (48.9 against 39.8% at 2.0 m and 49.5 against 39.2% at 2.66 m). A wider SUT thus leaves less room for a fair failure under the same fairness condition, and width-randomised training recovers little of it. The one-seed probe had suggested a larger gain on the Tesla, from 64.5 to 79.0%, which the three seeds did not confirm.

**Deceleration and cut-out scenarios.** The CARLA re-executions of Section S5 were rerun with the corrected harness, with the same plans, followers and analysis. In the deceleration scenarios CARLA's SUT collided in 91.5% of the 165 robust failures (stored 93.3%), and C avoided all of them, now also without its AEB layer (stored 88.5%, below the 90% criterion). In the cut-out scenarios C avoided all 400 (stored 96.5%), and the SUT collided in 90.0% (stored 91.0%). Each follower's one-dimensional verdict agreed with CARLA in at least 92.6% of the plans, whereas the stored run had 85.5% for the IDM follower with a 1 s delay, below 90%. The earlier rejections thus came from the harness; Table S12 keeps the stored verdicts as recorded and adds the rerun values.

**Three attack shapes.** A figure in an earlier draft of the paper posed three stored fair failures of different shape in CARLA from the stored states: (a) development case 232, (b) and (c) case 231 started behind the follower. The figure was later replaced by one with two shapes that hold with CARLA physics (section "Attack shapes of the former Fig. 7, run with CARLA physics" below), and that figure was removed from the paper on 2026-11-16. With the design fixed before the run, the three episodes were then run with CARLA physics against CARLA-simulated followers. With the earlier harness the SUT collided in (a) and (b), at 8.5 and 7.0 s (highway-env: 8.4 and 6.6 s), and C avoided all three, as the design required. Rerun with the corrected harness, the SUT still collided in (b) at 7.0 s but avoided (a) by 0.36 m, so the criterion of the design was no longer met. C avoided all three, with minimum gaps of 6.3, 0.5 and 2.5 m (highway-env: 6.8, 0.6 and 3.0 m), although in (b) the bodies came within 0.45 m, inside C's 0.5 m margin. Two further runs gave the same outcomes. In (c) the behaviour-cloned SUT has no CARLA implementation, so only C was run. The attack of (a) is thus one of the stored attacks that CARLA's SUT avoids when they are replayed unchanged.

### S13.6 Autoware on exiD (B80)

The agent's policy stage (three seeds) and screened refinement, and the collision-seeking baseline, were run against AW-default and AW-r157 on the 120 exiD cut-ins of the pre-specified evaluation, with the harness of the development run (Section S4.1). Autoware had not been run on exiD, but the cases had been used once, so the experiment is a post-hoc reuse; the hypotheses were fixed before the run with the thresholds of the development run: in each setting at most 5% of the policy stage's collisions reference-unavoidable (AX1) and at least 80% of the baseline's (AX2), confirmed detection under AW-default above that under AW-r157 with a lower bound above 0 (AX3), confirmed detection under AW-r157 of at least 10% (AX4), and at least 80% of the reruns of the reported failures colliding (AX5). AX1 to AX4 were supported: none of the policy stage's 246 and 190 collisions was reference-unavoidable, 271 of 273 and 269 of 270 of the baseline's were, and confirmed detection was 73.3% [65.8, 80.6] and 51.1% [43.3, 58.9], a difference of 22.2 points [16.1, 28.6] (development: 90.9 and 77.4%; earlier version on MiTra, post hoc: 58.5 and 36.2%). AX5 was supported for AW-default (91.1% of 1,020 reruns collided) and rejected for AW-r157 (66.3% of 820): of its 205 reported failures, 104 collided in all four reruns and 45 in none, so with C's braking limits more failures lie near Autoware's capability boundary, where its non-deterministic timing decides. The confirmed rates count only failures that collided in at least two of four reruns, so the rejection qualifies the reported, not the confirmed, failures. The policy stage produced 226 of the 281 reported failures under AW-default and 159 of 245 under AW-r157 (a median of one Autoware query per detection); 91.4 and 86.3% of the reported failures were consensus-fair, and none was graded unavoidable. Autoware began braking 0.4 s after the cut-in became visible (C: 0.9 s) and reached a median peak of −2.1 and −3.6 m/s² (C: −7.59 m/s²); the minimal-risk manoeuvre engaged in 27.5 and 6.3% of the failures. No episode, refinement or rerun failed; the closed-loop decisions again exceeded one tick at most (0.55 and 0.72 s), which the open-loop reruns check.

### S13.7 Controls derived from C on exiD (B81)

The ten controls of Section S5, C and the variants with one factor changed, were run against the final agent on the 120 exiD cut-ins of the pre-specified evaluation. These SUTs had not been run on exiD, but the cases had been used once, so the experiment is a post-hoc reuse; the composition, the analysis and B61's hypotheses were fixed before the run: C is never detected (K1), the two stronger variants are detected in at most 3% of the cases (K2), and detection within 37 queries does not decrease along any weakened factor, with the weakest level's lower bound above 0 (K3). All three were supported. C, reaction 0.5 s and braking 8.5 m/s² were never detected at 1, 6 or 37 queries. Within 37 queries, a reaction delay of +0.25, +0.5 and +1.0 s was detected in 75.0, 80.8 and 81.7% of the cases, braking limits of 6.5 and 5.5 m/s² in 43.1 and 68.1%, and perception at 1.5 and 2.0 m in 78.6 and 81.7%; the weakest levels' lower bounds are 74.2, 59.2 and 74.2%. The policy stage alone, with one query, separates the steps more clearly (reaction 12.8, 50.8 and 67.8%, braking 3.6 and 27.8%, perception 27.2 and 61.1%). The order is that of the development cases (Section S5), whereas the level at which reaction and perception saturate is lower, about 82% against 92–94%: it is close to the 80.8% of following H0 on the same cases (Section 6.2), so it reflects the share of cases with enough attack authority for a fair and realistic attack. The four runs passed the replay gate and a pre-check that reproduced six stored policy rows of the pre-specified evaluation, with no episode or refinement error, and no policy-stage collision was reference-unavoidable.

### From the earlier draft's Section 6.6, External software and simulators (now Section 6.5)

**Autoware on the development cases.** To examine whether the agent applies to an external automated driving stack, we tested two configurations of Autoware Universe on the 62 development cases without retraining the agent. In the planning simulator, ground-truth objects replaced sensor-based perception, while tracking, prediction, planning, control, AEB and the MRM were kept.  Because Autoware does not run deterministically, the premise of Proposition 1 (Section 4.3) that execution and replay agree holds only approximately. We therefore treated a share of at most 5% reference-unavoidable collisions among the policy stage's SUT collisions as a hypothesis, fixed before the run and then judged, rather than as a property guaranteed by construction. None of the 305 SUT collisions of the policy stage was reference-unavoidable, whereas 327 of the 329 collisions (99.4%) of the collision-seeking baseline were. The screened refinement sent no inadmissible plan to Autoware either (0 of 915 queries).  What was fixed before the first Autoware run, however, is the policy stage that the agent shares with the earlier version; the screening was designed in response to the inadmissible queries observed in that run. We therefore separate the evidence for the policy's transfer to external software from the in-sample results of the screened refinement on the same development cases.

To separate first reports from reproduced failures of this non-deterministic stack, we replayed each stored adversary trajectory open-loop four times. A failure counted as confirmed only if it collided in at least two of the reruns. AW-default keeps the as-shipped braking, whereas AW-r157 applies C's emergency braking limits but keeps the controller limits as shipped. The agent confirmed failures in 90.9% [83.3, 96.8] of the cases with AW-default and in 77.4% [68.3, 86.0] with AW-r157. The difference between the two settings was 13.4 points [7.5, 20.4], a case-bootstrap interval; the development data come from only three recordings, too few for a recording-cluster interval. The policy stage's first query produced 157 of the 171 reported failures with AW-default and 126 of the 159 with AW-r157. Reproduction rates in the confirmation reruns were 0.99 and 0.89, the held-out reference drivers agreed with the verdict on 91.6% and 81.4% of the failures, and no failure was graded unavoidable under Annex 5 of UN R157. With a tracker that coasts the object at constant velocity through the misses, the stack avoided nearly all of these cut-ins, and its braking onset did not move. ### From the earlier draft's Section 6.7, Learning, screening and query cost (now Section 6.4)

At the full budget of 36–37 queries, the agent and the screened GP-BO alone were within 4 points of each other on every dataset and SUT group (Table S6). For BC-highD and ADS 2.0 m, the policy's first query gave a small advantage or did worse than the screened GP-BO alone. On NGSIM, where the adversary lacks the speed headroom to slow down enough, both methods detected failures in 6.4% of the cases. What learning provides is therefore not a higher final detection across all SUTs and cases, but early savings of expensive SUT queries in traffic with enough attack authority, and closed-loop execution of the policy under the shield. Under the stricter realism and naturalistic braking conditions of Section 6.3, the policy's first query rarely met the success contract, and the refinement carried most of the detection. We also retrained the policy with the same recipe against the training panel plus 60 drivers built on C's structure, each with perception, reaction or braking weakened. One-query detection on the five detectable SUTs outside the training panel then fell from 24.8% to 22.8% (difference −2.0 points [−4.6, 0.4]). This experiment on the development cases had its design and improvement criterion fixed before training, and it missed the required gain of 10 points. A larger training population alone did not extend the one-query advantage to SUTs of other designs, and for such SUTs the refinement was an important complement.

**Contributions of screening and the search model.** On the development cases, the earlier version's unscreened refinement spent 59% of its 34,077 SUT queries on inadmissible plans, and 2,460 of the SUT collisions during that search were collisions that C could not avoid either. On Autoware, 128 of the 204 collisions of the earlier version's unscreened refinement were reference-unavoidable, although none of them entered the reported failures.  The screened refinement, by contrast, run alone on every development case and SUT, executed 28,410 SUT queries, none of them inadmissible, at an average of 142 C replays per SUT query. Within the same budget of 37 queries, it raised detection by 17.5 points on average over the nine detectable SUTs, with per-SUT changes from 0 to +55.9 points.  The additional failures of ADS 2.0 m, where the gain was largest, are almost all light contacts (Section 6.3). Among the few new failures of competent SUTs, those of AEB H0 and of the learned SUT without delay exceeded the 3% bound fixed before this development comparison; Section 6.2 describes these failures. This comparison evaluates the earlier and the final version on the same development data and is in-sample.

Screening, which checks admissibility before the query, and the search model, which chooses the next plan among the admitted candidates, made separate contributions. Under the same admission rule and within 36 refinement queries, screened random search found failures of the weakest SUT, following H0, in 48.4% of the cases and of ADS 2.0 m in 3.2%. The screened GP-BO found them in 87.1% and 69.4%. Behind the same C-based shield, a collision-seeking policy had no reference-unavoidable collision in 3,162 policy episodes, but it found one-query failures of the weakest SUTs in only 5.4% of the cases. Keeping attacks fair and detecting failures effectively are thus different roles. The shield excludes reference-unavoidable collisions when the premises of Proposition 1 (Section 4.3) hold, whereas the reward relative to the reference driver and the GP-BO's choice of candidates help to find avoidable failures. Finally, we ran the refinement on the development cases with two further random streams, with the design fixed before the run. They moved P1 by at most 1.6 points and the mean detection of the weak ladder by at most 1.5 points. The standard deviation of P1 over the three streams (0.9 points) was about a quarter of its case-bootstrap standard error (3.7 points), but the default confidence intervals do not include the variation of the refinement's random stream.

Computational cost, too, has to be assessed separately for SUT queries and for replays of the reference driver C (Table S7). Policy training took 2.25 M steps and 1.4–1.8 h per seed, and the shield used 15.8 C replays per attack on average (at most 84). In the tests of the 62 development cases against the 17 main SUTs, the agent used 26,699 SUT queries and 3.70 M C replays, whereas the screened GP-BO alone used 28,410 and 4.04 M. Because a successful first policy query ends the test without refinement, the agent used about 6% fewer SUT queries and about 8% fewer C replays. In this development campaign, training three seeds (about 1.6 h each) cost less than the roughly 336,000 C replays saved (about 37 h in a single process), so the agent was cheaper whatever an SUT query costs. That screening removes inadmissible SUT queries does not, however, guarantee a shorter total run time. On Autoware, the C replays ran serially on the stack's instance. Screening therefore lengthened the refinement's instance time by about 40% with AW-default (from 1.57 to 2.20 h) and by about 31% with AW-r157 (from 4.52 to 5.92 h). Because C replays can run in parallel, comparisons of actual run time depend on the execution environment and the computing resources. In this experiment, the savings in SUT queries and the change in actual run time therefore did not necessarily point the same way.

## Controlled ablation of the policy stage (2026-11-14 and 15)

This ablation answers an external review that asked for the agent's parts to be removed one at a time from the final model.

**Design.** The design was fixed before the runs on 2026-11-14 at 16:14 (B83, design available on request). The final agent's policy stage was retrained with three seeds per arm, and each arm changes one part:
- *collision reward*: +1 for a valid contact, +0.3 for proximity (TTC below 1.5 s) and −1 for an invalid attack, instead of the reward based on C's verdict; the shield and commitment are kept;
- *commitment (G1) off* in initialisation, fine-tuning and testing;
- *G1 off after initialisation*, that is, in fine-tuning and testing only;
- *G2 off* in the reward, with commitment kept.

Every arm, including the stored final agent, was tested with and without the shield. The refinement was kept fixed at its stored results. Values within 37 queries are therefore the union of an arm's policy stage and the stored refinement, and they cannot fall below the refinement alone.

All arms were judged with the paper's success contract (Section 3.4). G1 was measured in every arm with the replay test, a retreat of more than 0.2 m after the adversary crosses C's perception boundary, including the arms where it holds by construction.

**Checks.**
- The re-evaluated final agent reproduced the stored results case by case: 1,054 pairs per development seed, and HIGH-SIM and exiD as well.
- The seed-1 policies without G1 and without G2 reproduced the stored policies of the same earlier design.
- No policy-stage collision was reference-unavoidable in any arm tested with the shield.

- Without commitment, longitudinal RSS collided while C avoided in 74.2% of the cases at the first query.
- In every one of those collisions the policy had withdrawn its lane goal, but the adversary never moved back by more than 0.2 m after crossing C's perception boundary. The replay test of G1 therefore excluded none of them.
